Recommendations

I recommend building the new SimpleRent website around a straightforward task: helping people find the right equipment, understand the rental, and book with confidence.

SimpleRent already has much of what makes a rental service useful: professional equipment, ready-made kits, practical advice, customer credit and people who can help with a production. The website should bring these strengths forward. At the moment, customers encounter broad category pages, competing messages and scattered rental information before they can make a clear choice.

The biggest improvement is therefore the structure. I recommend clear entry points for equipment, kits and studios, useful homepage search, and dates, availability, price and included equipment presented together. A consistent visual style and restrained motion can make the experience feel reliable.

These recommendations are for the custom platform SimpleRent is planning. They do not depend on keeping WordPress or WooCommerce, and they do not prescribe the new technology stack. The design details below are recommendations for discussion, rather than decisions already approved by SimpleRent.

1. Make the homepage useful immediately

The current homepage establishes a filmmaking mood, but its opening photograph and headline do not give visitors a direct action. Contact us gets the strongest button treatment in the header, while equipment discovery starts in navigation or further down the page. Featured products are currently a narrow selection of anamorphic lenses rather than an introduction to the wider range.

I recommend keeping the real production photography and adding:

  • A short explanation of the offer and location: film equipment rental, with pickup in Hvidovre and delivery where available. I would confirm delivery coverage before publishing that promise.
  • A prominent equipment search and Browse equipment action.
  • A search field with useful examples such as FX3 or a 100 W light, plus a glimpse of the next section so visitors see there is more to explore. A subtle rotating example is an option; keep a permanent label and stop motion while typing or when reduced motion is preferred.
  • Direct links to the main equipment types and a separate route to complete kits.

I recommend letting customers browse without entering dates and asking for dates when they choose to add rental gear. Carry that selection forward to the next item to reduce repeated entry, but let customers change dates for each item in the basket - for example, to keep one camera longer. Before dates are chosen, say Check availability, rather than promising that equipment is available. The optional date filter remains available for people who want to narrow their search early. Show rental days and collection/return windows separately.

Below the first screen, I would show the main equipment types, selected kits, a short explanation of how renting works, and practical reasons to choose SimpleRent. Studios and helpful guides can follow. Featured gear should serve a clear purpose - popular choices, new arrivals or a particular production - rather than imply that three specialist lenses represent the whole catalogue.

The site-wide notice, cookie banner and promotional popup should not compete for attention. The mobile review reports that these can cover a substantial part of the screen together. I recommend one interruption at a time and leaving promotional popups out of the initial visit. Moving a popup to exit intent is an option, not an automatic solution.

Give each product one main subcategory, then connect it to relevant kits and compatible extras. Lens mount, brand and specifications should be filters, not extra layers of navigation. Audio recorders belong with audio; monitor-recorders with monitors. Rental batteries belong under power, disposable batteries under consumables. Items can be available to rent, buy or both; only show the options SimpleRent actually offers.

Keep the route to a product short: Rent gear → group → subcategory → product, with direct search and menu shortcuts. The latest sitemap review reports 765 products across 94 categories, with some addresses up to seven levels deep. If Frederik wants to proceed with this structure, I recommend checking the full catalogue against it before implementation. The detailed mapping can then be scoped with whoever will maintain the product data.

/rent/ is the working address. Confirm whether /gear/ would better describe a catalogue that also contains things to buy. The five sections are the proposed direction; their final labels and active offers still need Frederik’s review. Generators should only appear if SimpleRent confirms an actual offer.

3. Make equipment easier to find and compare

I recommend useful filters and one consistent sort menu across listings and search results. Keep exact model matches ahead of accessories, retain the typed query, and recognize names such as FX6, Sony FX6 and ILME-FX6 as the same search. Suggestions can include products, gear groups and kits.

Across the catalogue: available for my dates, Rent/Buy, a price range and brand. Rental prices use a price-per-day slider in DKK with the VAT basis clearly stated. In Buy mode, show purchase prices instead. Date availability applies to rental items; purchase items need their own stock indication.

Gear group Useful filters
Cameras and optics Lens mount; focal-length range; sensor size/coverage; lens type and T-stop; resolution and recording format
Lighting and grip Wattage; colour/daylight/bi-colour/RGB; battery power; light type and mount
Audio and monitoring Microphone type and channels; screen size; brightness in nits; connections; recording capability
Support, motion and power Payload; battery mount; cable connector and length
Live and streaming Inputs and connections, including SDI, HDMI and NDI where relevant
Media and consumables Card type and capacity

Use range sliders for values such as focal length, price, payload, screen size and cable length, with editable number fields. Only show filters relevant to the selected products. Wattage describes power, not directly how much light a fixture produces; label it honestly and add comparable measured output only where reliable specifications exist.

Sort by: Recommended (default), Most rented in the last 90 days, Price low to high, Price high to low, Newest, and Name A-Z. Recommended can combine staff choices, popularity and availability once dates are selected. Most rented must use actual bookings; buy-only items should not pretend to have rental popularity.

Selected filters appear as removable labels above the results, with Clear all. Show result counts, keep filter and sort choices in the page address so links can be shared and the back button works, and update results without a full reload. On mobile, open filters in a panel from the bottom with a Show … items button. When nothing matches, explain which filters to loosen and offer relevant alternatives.

Product cards should show the model, a useful configuration detail, a clearly qualified price and See dates & price or Check availability. Equipment lists collect ideas; they should never look like a confirmed reservation.

4. Bring the booking information together

Product pages already show rental dates, availability and prices for different periods. That is a useful foundation. The presentation should make it easier to answer four questions:

  1. Is this the right equipment for my production?
  2. Exactly what comes with it?
  3. Is it available for my rental days, and what is the total?
  4. When do I collect and return it, and what happens when I continue?

I recommend a compact booking area containing dates, quantity, availability, total and the main action. A sticky panel may help on desktop; on mobile, a small price/action summary could preserve context. Both need testing to ensure they do not cover content or force a decision before people understand the equipment.

Use consistent pricing labels, including VAT and duration. Put specifications in a scannable table rather than widely spaced paragraphs. List included items, required extras and optional additions separately. Basic and Advanced configurations should explain their differences without making customers infer them from photographs.

For kits, preserve the existing result examples and setup guidance. The Interview Kit is a strong example of explaining what someone can make with the equipment. Its visible contents list should be checked against the description, which also mentions lighting. Show quantities and exclusions, then link to compatible additions. A customer-built kit configurator can wait until there is a clear need and reliable component availability.

I recommend a Pickup or delivery choice in the basket, with the delivery charge calculated and shown before the customer confirms the order. Product pages can indicate delivery eligibility where restrictions matter, without adding another decision to every page. Resources and support can explain coverage, timing and extra charges. I would confirm these rules with SimpleRent before presenting delivery as available for every order.

I also recommend a quiet You might also need section with genuinely compatible extras: the right memory card for a camera, a boom arm for a microphone, or a stand and extension cable for a light. This can help customers prepare a complete setup without interrupting their browsing.

When the basket already contains several parts of a kit, I recommend suggesting the complete kit as an alternative. For example, a microphone and boom arm might lead to an interview audio kit with a recorder, stand and wind protection. Show the actual contents and price difference, and let the customer choose whether to replace the separate items. Nothing is added or replaced automatically, and compatibility and availability need checking for the selected dates.

5. Explain how renting works before customers need to ask

I recommend replacing the FAQ-first introduction with a simple sequence:

Choose equipment and rental days → review availability, total and pickup or delivery → confirm the booking.

Place the important practical details alongside that explanation, with more specific questions below. Pickup and return, business payment arrangements, insurance and damage should have their own clearly labeled sections that can be linked directly from relevant pages.

The current advance-booking messages need clarification: the banner asks for two days before pickup, while the FAQ says bookings must be at least one day ahead. The current working understanding is that two days is preferred and one-day requests may sometimes be possible, without a guarantee. Frederik still needs to confirm this before we state it as policy.

The FAQ also describes a five-minute cart hold. If that continues, customers should see when it begins, when it expires and how to recover without losing their equipment selection. None of those transactional behaviors were tested during this review.

Review old or unclear copy, including the Covid section and “Sluse” terminology. Explain self-service collection in plain English, but confirm whether “locker” accurately describes the facility. Remove outdated information only after the operating policy is checked.

6. Give spaces, services and resources a proper home

I recommend giving Studios and spaces a distinct destination, separate from crew and production services. Rooms are currently buried in equipment categories, while crew and post-production are described in an article.

For each space, show size, capacity, acoustics, access hours, included equipment and extra charges. Explore direct booking once the room calendar and operating rules are confirmed. A simple upper-floor plan could help visitors understand where the room is, how large it is and where to go. Confirm which rooms actually exist: the sampled Simple Studio says it is not sound isolated, so it should not be presented as a soundproof podcast room. Crew roles and post-production need a clear enquiry route; I recommend offering pickup or delivery in the basket, rather than listing delivery as a separate production service.

Resources and support combines rental help with a small, useful collection of guides. It can feel complete without promising a constant stream of new articles. Start with around nine practical guides, especially one for each core kit: setup, basic lighting, LOG recording, simple colour grading, studio setup, audio and backups. Reuse good existing material. Prepare unfinished pages privately and publish only useful, complete content; do not create empty categories for one article each.

I recommend removing the one-episode podcast from the promoted offer unless there is a clear plan to continue it. Review the courses that still show early-2024 dates: show current courses only if they are genuinely offered. A video library can come later. Keep social links, but favour a simple Instagram link over a large automatically mirrored feed unless that feed has a clear purpose.

7. Refine the visual style and interaction

I recommend keeping the recognizable black, white, purple and lime identity, unless Frederik wants a different direction. Use those colours deliberately and keep the layout simple, with minimal motion and subtle expressive touches.

The design review identifies weak contrast in several colour combinations, particularly lime text on light backgrounds and white text on bright buttons. Use lime where it has strong contrast, such as against dark surfaces, and dark text on lime buttons. Check each actual pairing rather than applying one colour rule everywhere. Keep purple as part of the identity, while consolidating inconsistent greens and greys. Give strong colours clear roles so notices do not overpower booking.

Create a clear type hierarchy and consistent product, price and button styles. Choose a readable, consistent font and let headings guide the eye without overwhelming the content.

Make keyboard navigation visible and mobile buttons comfortable to tap. Check date fields, quantity controls, menus and forms as carefully as the main buttons. Page headings should clearly describe the page, and availability should say what is available for the selected dates without relying on colour alone.

Menus, the basket and saved-list actions should respond quickly. Keep motion minimal, let people interrupt it naturally, and respect their preference for reduced motion. Remove unnecessary header and FAQ animation, and avoid first-visit promotions that cover the content. The detailed measurements and template checks remain in the supporting review.

8. Preserve the business behind the website

Moving away from WooCommerce does not remove the need for reliable availability, rental pricing, payments, invoices, customer permissions and credit. Existing credit balances and approved business payment arrangements need deliberate treatment. Returning customers should be able to understand their next rental, access relevant records and reuse equipment selections with a fresh price and availability check.

I recommend that the development and operations team confirm which system owns inventory, bookings, customer data and prices, and whether Rentman will remain. Migration rehearsals and complete booking tests belong in their implementation plan, including expired holds, failed payments, cancellations and credit adjustments.

The technical audit holds the detail. It is supporting material for the development team, rather than required reading for understanding this recommendation.

9. What I propose next

I recommend presenting and discussing this direction with Frederik first, then agreeing whether and where I can help with the next stage. The priorities I would lead with are simpler categories and filtering, better visibility for spaces and services, and a clearer path from choosing equipment to booking it.

If Frederik wants to explore the direction visually, I will develop selected UI mockups - for example, the homepage, a filtered equipment listing, a product page or the basket - once we agree which screens would be most useful. Those examples can make the recommendations easier to discuss before committing to a larger design scope.

Catalogue restructuring, product mapping and redirects may be useful later, but they are not promised deliverables at this stage. I can explore whether I can help with that work once the available data, development team and responsibilities are clear. Implementation, integration and migration need their own agreed scope.

Points to agree with Frederik

  • Direction and active offers: does the five-section structure fit the business, and which studios, crew roles and kit contents can be presented confidently? The catalogue address can be settled as part of that review.
  • Booking and delivery: confirm the advance-booking rule, delivery coverage and charges, and how pickup, delivery and different rental periods would work together.
  • Next steps and responsibilities: would visuals or UI mockups help, and where would Frederik like my involvement? Who will handle product information, platform decisions and the existing booking, payment and credit arrangements?

These recommendations describe the direction I would take. Further design or implementation work will depend on what Frederik wants to pursue and the scope we agree.
